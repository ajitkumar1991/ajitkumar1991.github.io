Quantum Information and Computation

No-cloning, state discrimination, entanglement, teleportation, and error correction

Core results in quantum information theory: the no-cloning and no-deleting theorems, quantum state discrimination, entanglement and teleportation, quantum error correction, and connections to tensor networks.
Author

Ajit Kumar Sorout

Published

March 23, 2026

Modified

March 24, 2026

Introduction

Quantum information theory asks a deceptively simple question: what can you do with quantum states that you cannot do with classical bits? The answer turns out to reshape our understanding of computation, communication, and even the structure of spacetime. These notes collect the core results that any working physicist should have at their fingertips — the no-cloning theorem, the limits of state discrimination, the structure of entanglement, and the formalism of quantum channels — before sketching how these ideas connect to tensor network methods and holography.

Throughout, we work with finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces \(\mathcal{H} \cong \mathbb{C}^d\) unless otherwise stated, and we set \(\hbar = 1\).

The No-Cloning Theorem

Statement

The no-cloning theorem is one of the earliest and most consequential results in quantum information. It asserts that there is no physical process that can duplicate an arbitrary unknown quantum state.

Theorem (Wootters–Zurek, Dieks, 1982). There exists no unitary operator \(U\) acting on \(\mathcal{H} \otimes \mathcal{H}\) such that, for every state \(|\psi\rangle \in \mathcal{H}\), \[ U \bigl(|\psi\rangle \otimes |0\rangle\bigr) = |\psi\rangle \otimes |\psi\rangle, \] where \(|0\rangle\) is some fixed blank state.

Proof

The proof is short and uses only two properties of quantum mechanics: linearity and the preservation of inner products by unitary evolution.

Suppose such a \(U\) exists. Take two states \(|\psi\rangle\) and \(|\phi\rangle\), and assume the cloning relation holds for both: \[ U(|\psi\rangle \otimes |0\rangle) = |\psi\rangle \otimes |\psi\rangle, \qquad U(|\phi\rangle \otimes |0\rangle) = |\phi\rangle \otimes |\phi\rangle. \]

Since \(U\) is unitary, it preserves inner products. Compute the overlap of the left-hand sides: \[ \bigl(\langle\psi| \otimes \langle 0|\bigr)\bigl(|\phi\rangle \otimes |0\rangle\bigr) = \langle\psi|\phi\rangle \cdot \langle 0|0\rangle = \langle\psi|\phi\rangle. \]

Now compute the overlap of the right-hand sides: \[ \bigl(\langle\psi| \otimes \langle\psi|\bigr)\bigl(|\phi\rangle \otimes |\phi\rangle\bigr) = \langle\psi|\phi\rangle^2. \]

Equating, we need \[ \langle\psi|\phi\rangle = \langle\psi|\phi\rangle^2, \] which forces \(\langle\psi|\phi\rangle \in \{0, 1\}\). So the cloning machine can only work if the two states are either identical or orthogonal — it cannot clone a pair of non-orthogonal states. Since a universal cloner must handle arbitrary states, no such \(U\) exists. \(\blacksquare\)

Physical implications

The no-cloning theorem is not merely a mathematical curiosity. It has profound physical consequences:

  1. Quantum key distribution. The security of protocols like BB84 rests on no-cloning: an eavesdropper cannot copy the transmitted quantum states without disturbing them, and this disturbance is detectable.

  2. Quantum error correction. Since we cannot copy quantum states, error correction in the quantum setting requires a fundamentally different approach from classical repetition codes. The trick is to encode information in entangled states across multiple qubits without ever learning (and thus collapsing) the logical state.

  3. No superluminal signalling. If cloning were possible, one could use it together with entanglement to send information faster than light. The no-cloning theorem is thus intimately connected to relativistic causality.

Approximate cloning

While perfect cloning is impossible, one can ask: how well can we clone? The optimal approximate cloner for qubits was found by Buzek and Hillery (1996). For a \(1 \to 2\) symmetric cloner acting on qubits, the optimal single-copy fidelity is \[ F = \frac{5}{6} \approx 0.833. \]

More generally, for a \(1 \to M\) cloner in dimension \(d\), the optimal fidelity is \[ F = \frac{M(d-1) + d + M}{M(d+1)}. \]

These bounds are tight and can be achieved by specific quantum circuits. The existence of a gap between the cloning fidelity and unity is another way of quantifying the “quantumness” of information.

The no-deleting theorem

The no-cloning theorem has a natural complement: the no-deleting theorem (Pati and Braunstein, 2000). While no-cloning says you cannot create a copy of an unknown quantum state, no-deleting says you cannot destroy one of two copies of an unknown state against a blank ancilla while keeping the other intact.

Theorem. There exists no linear operator \(U\) acting on \(\mathcal{H} \otimes \mathcal{H} \otimes \mathcal{H}_A\) such that, for every \(|\psi\rangle\), \[ U\bigl(|\psi\rangle \otimes |\psi\rangle \otimes |A\rangle\bigr) = |\psi\rangle \otimes |0\rangle \otimes |A'\rangle, \] where \(|0\rangle\) is a fixed state and \(|A'\rangle\) may depend on \(|\psi\rangle\).

The proof strategy mirrors that of no-cloning. If such a \(U\) existed, taking two input states \(|\psi\rangle\) and \(|\phi\rangle\) and computing inner products of both sides yields \[ \langle\psi|\phi\rangle^2 \langle A|A\rangle = \langle\psi|\phi\rangle \langle 0|0\rangle \langle A'_\psi|A'_\phi\rangle, \] which simplifies to \(\langle\psi|\phi\rangle = \langle A'_\psi|A'_\phi\rangle\). This means the “deleted” information has been transferred entirely into the ancilla, not destroyed — quantum information is never lost, only moved. This is a direct manifestation of unitarity: since quantum time evolution is reversible, information can be scrambled or redistributed but never erased.

Together, no-cloning and no-deleting express a deep principle: quantum information is conserved. You can move it, encode it, spread it across entangled subsystems, but you can neither duplicate it nor annihilate it. This conservation law is closely related to the unitarity of quantum mechanics and has direct implications for the black hole information paradox, where the question is precisely whether information thrown into a black hole is truly destroyed or eventually recovered.

Quantum State Discrimination

The problem

Suppose Alice prepares one of two quantum states \(\rho_1\) or \(\rho_2\) with prior probabilities \(p_1\) and \(p_2 = 1 - p_1\), and sends it to Bob. Bob must perform a measurement and guess which state was sent. If \(\rho_1\) and \(\rho_2\) are orthogonal, Bob can distinguish them perfectly. But if they are non-orthogonal, errors are unavoidable. The question is: what is the minimum probability of error?

Minimum error discrimination: the Helstrom bound

The optimal strategy is described by a two-outcome POVM \(\{E_1, E_2\}\) with \(E_1 + E_2 = \mathbb{I}\), \(E_i \geq 0\). The probability of error is \[ P_{\rm err} = p_1 \, \mathrm{Tr}(\rho_1 E_2) + p_2 \, \mathrm{Tr}(\rho_2 E_1). \]

Theorem (Helstrom, 1976). The minimum error probability for discriminating between \(\rho_1\) and \(\rho_2\) with priors \(p_1, p_2\) is \[ P_{\rm err} = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(1 - \|p_1 \rho_1 - p_2 \rho_2\|_1\bigr), \] where \(\|A\|_1 = \mathrm{Tr}\sqrt{A^\dagger A}\) is the trace norm.

The optimal POVM is obtained by diagonalizing \(\Lambda = p_1 \rho_1 - p_2 \rho_2\) and assigning \(E_1\) to the positive eigenspace and \(E_2\) to the negative eigenspace.

Example: two pure qubit states. For \(|\psi_1\rangle\) and \(|\psi_2\rangle\) with equal priors (\(p_1 = p_2 = 1/2\)) and overlap \(|\langle\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle| = \cos\theta\), the Helstrom bound gives \[ P_{\rm err} = \frac{1}{2}(1 - \sin\theta). \]

When \(\theta = \pi/2\) (orthogonal states), we get \(P_{\rm err} = 0\). When \(\theta = 0\) (identical states), \(P_{\rm err} = 1/2\) — pure guessing. Everything in between interpolates smoothly.

Unambiguous discrimination

An alternative strategy is unambiguous state discrimination (USD): Bob is allowed to sometimes output “I don’t know,” but when he does give an answer, it must be correct with certainty. For two pure states with equal priors, the optimal failure probability (the probability of the inconclusive outcome) is \[ P_{\rm fail} = |\langle\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle|. \]

This was first derived by Ivanovic (1987), Dieks (1988), and Peres (1988). USD is only possible when the states have linearly independent support; for two states with overlapping support (mixed states whose supports are not orthogonal), unambiguous discrimination may be impossible.

Relation to no-cloning

State discrimination and no-cloning are deeply connected. If we could clone, we could make many copies of the unknown state and perform tomography, distinguishing any two states with arbitrarily small error. The Helstrom bound is thus a quantitative expression of the same physics that forbids cloning.

Worked example: discriminating \(|0\rangle\) and a rotated state

Consider the concrete problem of discriminating \(|\psi_1\rangle = |0\rangle\) and \(|\psi_2\rangle = \cos\theta\,|0\rangle + \sin\theta\,|1\rangle\) with equal priors \(p_1 = p_2 = 1/2\). We compute the Helstrom bound and the optimal measurement explicitly.

Step 1: form the Helstrom operator. Define \(\Lambda = p_1|\psi_1\rangle\langle\psi_1| - p_2|\psi_2\rangle\langle\psi_2|\). In the \(\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}\) basis: \[ \Lambda = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} - \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta \\ \sin\theta \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 - \cos^2\theta & -\cos\theta\sin\theta \\ -\cos\theta\sin\theta & -\sin^2\theta \end{pmatrix}. \] Using \(1 - \cos^2\theta = \sin^2\theta\), this simplifies to \[ \Lambda = \frac{\sin\theta}{2}\begin{pmatrix} \sin\theta & -\cos\theta \\ -\cos\theta & -\sin\theta \end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 2: diagonalize \(\Lambda\). The matrix in parentheses has eigenvalues \(\pm 1\) (it is traceless with determinant \(-\sin^2\theta - \cos^2\theta = -1\)), so the eigenvalues of \(\Lambda\) are \(\pm \frac{\sin\theta}{2}\). The trace norm is therefore \[ \|\Lambda\|_1 = \frac{\sin\theta}{2} + \frac{\sin\theta}{2} = \sin\theta. \]

Step 3: the Helstrom bound. The minimum error probability is \[ P_{\rm err} = \frac{1}{2}(1 - \sin\theta), \] matching the general formula for two pure states with overlap \(|\langle\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle| = \cos\theta\).

Step 4: the optimal POVM. The eigenvector of \(\Lambda\) with positive eigenvalue \(+\frac{\sin\theta}{2}\) is \[ |v_+\rangle = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\,|0\rangle - \sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,|1\rangle, \] and the eigenvector with negative eigenvalue is \[ |v_-\rangle = \sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,|0\rangle + \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\,|1\rangle. \] The optimal POVM is the projective measurement in this basis: \(E_1 = |v_+\rangle\langle v_+|\) (guess \(\psi_1\)) and \(E_2 = |v_-\rangle\langle v_-|\) (guess \(\psi_2\)). Note that the measurement basis lies along the angle bisector of the two states on the Bloch sphere — this is geometrically intuitive, as the optimal discriminator should be oriented to maximally separate the two signal states.

As a check: when \(\theta = \pi/4\), the states \(|0\rangle\) and \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)\) give \(P_{\rm err} = \frac{1}{2}(1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}) \approx 0.146\), and the measurement is performed in the basis tilted at \(\pi/8\) from the computational basis.

Entanglement

Bell states

The maximally entangled states of two qubits are the Bell states: \[ |\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle), \qquad |\Phi^-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle - |11\rangle), \] \[ |\Psi^+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|01\rangle + |10\rangle), \qquad |\Psi^-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|01\rangle - |10\rangle). \]

These form an orthonormal basis for \(\mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^2\). Any of the four can be obtained from any other by a local unitary on one qubit. The reduced density matrix of each Bell state is \(\rho_A = \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{I}\) — maximally mixed.

Schmidt decomposition

Any bipartite pure state \(|\psi\rangle \in \mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_B\) can be written as \[ |\psi\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \lambda_i \, |a_i\rangle \otimes |b_i\rangle, \] where \(\{|a_i\rangle\}\) and \(\{|b_i\rangle\}\) are orthonormal sets in \(\mathcal{H}_A\) and \(\mathcal{H}_B\) respectively, \(\lambda_i > 0\), and \(\sum_i \lambda_i^2 = 1\). The number \(r\) is the Schmidt rank, and the \(\lambda_i\) are the Schmidt coefficients.

The Schmidt decomposition is simply the singular value decomposition (SVD) applied to the coefficient matrix \(C_{ij}\) in any product basis expansion \(|\psi\rangle = \sum_{ij} C_{ij} |i\rangle|j\rangle\). A state is entangled if and only if its Schmidt rank is greater than 1.

Von Neumann entropy as an entanglement measure

For a bipartite pure state \(|\psi\rangle_{AB}\) with reduced density matrix \(\rho_A = \mathrm{Tr}_B(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)\), the entanglement entropy is the von Neumann entropy of the reduced state: \[ S(\rho_A) = -\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_A \log \rho_A) = -\sum_i \lambda_i^2 \log \lambda_i^2, \] where \(\lambda_i\) are the Schmidt coefficients. This is the unique measure of bipartite pure-state entanglement (up to normalization) satisfying natural axioms: it vanishes for product states, is maximized for maximally entangled states, and does not increase under local operations and classical communication (LOCC).

For a maximally entangled state of two \(d\)-dimensional systems, \(S = \log d\).

Entanglement of formation

For mixed states, quantifying entanglement is harder. The entanglement of formation \(E_F(\rho)\) is defined as the minimum average entanglement over all pure-state decompositions: \[ E_F(\rho) = \min_{\{p_i, |\psi_i\rangle\}} \sum_i p_i \, S(\mathrm{Tr}_B |\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|), \] where the minimum is over all ensembles \(\{p_i, |\psi_i\rangle\}\) such that \(\rho = \sum_i p_i |\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|\).

For two qubits, Wootters (1998) found an elegant closed-form expression for \(E_F\) in terms of the concurrence \(C(\rho)\): \[ E_F = h\!\left(\frac{1 + \sqrt{1-C^2}}{2}\right), \] where \(h(x) = -x\log x - (1-x)\log(1-x)\) is the binary entropy. The concurrence can be computed from the eigenvalues of \(\rho(\sigma_y \otimes \sigma_y)\rho^*(\sigma_y \otimes \sigma_y)\).

Monogamy of entanglement

Entanglement is monogamous: if qubit \(A\) is maximally entangled with qubit \(B\), it cannot be entangled with a third qubit \(C\) at all. Quantitatively, the Coffman–Kundu–Wootters (CKW) inequality states that for three qubits, \[ C^2_{A|BC} \geq C^2_{AB} + C^2_{AC}, \] where \(C_{AB}\) is the concurrence of the reduced state \(\rho_{AB}\), and \(C_{A|BC}\) is the concurrence between \(A\) and the joint system \(BC\).

Monogamy is a distinctly quantum phenomenon with no classical analogue — classical correlations can be freely shared. It plays a central role in the security proofs of quantum key distribution and, remarkably, also appears in black hole physics through the “monogamy vs. ER=EPR” discussion.

Quantum teleportation

Quantum teleportation (Bennett et al., 1993) is the protocol that most vividly demonstrates the power of entanglement as a resource. It allows Alice to transmit an unknown qubit state \(|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\) to Bob using one shared Bell pair and two classical bits — without any quantum channel between them at the time of transmission.

Setup. Alice and Bob share the Bell state \(|\Phi^+\rangle_{AB} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)_{AB}\). Alice also holds the state \(|\psi\rangle_Q\) she wishes to teleport. The total initial state of the three qubits \(Q\), \(A\), \(B\) is \[ |\Psi_0\rangle = |\psi\rangle_Q \otimes |\Phi^+\rangle_{AB} = (\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle)_Q \otimes \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)_{AB}. \]

Step 1: rewrite in the Bell basis. The key algebraic identity is the expansion of a product basis in terms of Bell states. For any two qubits \(Q\) and \(A\): \[ |0\rangle_Q|0\rangle_A = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\Phi^+\rangle + |\Phi^-\rangle), \qquad |0\rangle_Q|1\rangle_A = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\Psi^+\rangle + |\Psi^-\rangle), \] \[ |1\rangle_Q|0\rangle_A = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\Psi^+\rangle - |\Psi^-\rangle), \qquad |1\rangle_Q|1\rangle_A = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|\Phi^+\rangle - |\Phi^-\rangle). \] Substituting and collecting terms, the full three-qubit state becomes \[ |\Psi_0\rangle = \frac{1}{2}\Bigl[|\Phi^+\rangle_{QA}(\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle)_B + |\Phi^-\rangle_{QA}(\alpha|0\rangle - \beta|1\rangle)_B \] \[ \quad + |\Psi^+\rangle_{QA}(\alpha|1\rangle + \beta|0\rangle)_B + |\Psi^-\rangle_{QA}(\alpha|1\rangle - \beta|0\rangle)_B\Bigr]. \]

Step 2: Bell measurement. Alice performs a joint measurement on qubits \(Q\) and \(A\) in the Bell basis, obtaining one of four equally likely outcomes. Each outcome leaves Bob’s qubit in one of four states related to \(|\psi\rangle\) by a known Pauli:

Alice’s outcome Bob’s state Correction
\(|\Phi^+\rangle\) \(\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\) \(\mathbb{I}\)
\(|\Phi^-\rangle\) \(\alpha|0\rangle - \beta|1\rangle\) \(\sigma_z\)
\(|\Psi^+\rangle\) \(\alpha|1\rangle + \beta|0\rangle\) \(\sigma_x\)
\(|\Psi^-\rangle\) \(\alpha|1\rangle - \beta|0\rangle\) \(i\sigma_y\)

Step 3: classical communication and correction. Alice sends her two-bit measurement outcome to Bob over a classical channel. Bob applies the corresponding Pauli correction to recover \(|\psi\rangle\) exactly.

Several features deserve emphasis. First, the protocol does not violate no-cloning: Alice’s original state is destroyed by the Bell measurement, so at no point do two copies coexist. Second, it does not allow faster-than-light communication: Bob’s qubit is in the maximally mixed state \(\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{I}\) until he receives Alice’s classical bits, so no information is available to him before the classical message arrives. Third, the protocol consumes the shared entanglement — after teleportation, the Bell pair is gone. Entanglement is thus a fungible resource: one ebit plus two classical bits equals one qubit of quantum communication.

Quantum Channels

Completely positive trace-preserving maps

A quantum channel is a physically realizable transformation of quantum states. Mathematically, it is a linear map \(\mathcal{E}: \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_A) \to \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_B)\) that is:

  1. Trace-preserving: \(\mathrm{Tr}[\mathcal{E}(\rho)] = \mathrm{Tr}(\rho)\) for all \(\rho\).
  2. Completely positive: \(\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathrm{id}_R\) maps positive operators to positive operators, for any reference system \(R\).

Complete positivity is stronger than positivity and is physically necessary: if Alice applies \(\mathcal{E}\) to her part of an entangled state, the joint state must remain a valid density matrix. The transpose map \(T(\rho) = \rho^T\) is positive but not completely positive — this is the basis of the Peres–Horodecki (PPT) entanglement criterion.

Kraus representation

Theorem (Stinespring, Kraus). A map \(\mathcal{E}\) is a quantum channel if and only if it admits a Kraus decomposition: \[ \mathcal{E}(\rho) = \sum_{k} K_k \rho K_k^\dagger, \qquad \sum_k K_k^\dagger K_k = \mathbb{I}. \]

The operators \(\{K_k\}\) are called Kraus operators. They are not unique: any set \(\{K_k'\} = \{U_{kl} K_l\}\) related by a unitary matrix gives the same channel.

Physically, the Kraus representation arises from coupling the system to an environment initially in a pure state \(|0\rangle_E\), applying a joint unitary \(U_{SE}\), and tracing out the environment: \[ \mathcal{E}(\rho) = \mathrm{Tr}_E\bigl[U(\rho \otimes |0\rangle\langle 0|)U^\dagger\bigr]. \]

The Kraus operators are \(K_k = \langle k|_E U |0\rangle_E\) for an orthonormal basis \(\{|k\rangle_E\}\).

Example: the depolarizing channel

The depolarizing channel replaces the input state with the maximally mixed state with probability \(p\): \[ \mathcal{E}_p(\rho) = (1-p)\rho + p \cdot \frac{\mathbb{I}}{d}. \]

For qubits (\(d = 2\)), this can be written in Kraus form using the Pauli matrices: \[ \mathcal{E}_p(\rho) = \left(1 - \frac{3p}{4}\right)\rho + \frac{p}{4}\bigl(\sigma_x \rho \sigma_x + \sigma_y \rho \sigma_y + \sigma_z \rho \sigma_z\bigr). \]

The depolarizing channel is a useful benchmark: it represents the “worst-case” noise model in the sense that it shrinks the Bloch sphere uniformly. It has quantum capacity zero for \(p \geq 1/2\) (for qubits), reflecting a threshold beyond which no quantum information can be reliably transmitted.

Quantum Error Correction

The basic idea

Classical information can be protected from noise by redundancy: encode a bit as \(0 \to 000\), \(1 \to 111\), and take a majority vote after transmission. For quantum information, this naive strategy fails — the no-cloning theorem forbids copying an unknown state \(|\psi\rangle\) into \(|\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle\). The key insight of quantum error correction (QEC) is that one can instead encode \(k\) logical qubits into \(n\) physical qubits by spreading the quantum information across entangled states, without ever learning or copying the logical state. The resulting \([[n,k,d]]\) code can correct any error affecting up to \(\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor\) physical qubits, where \(d\) is the code distance.

The 3-qubit bit-flip code

The simplest quantum code corrects a single bit-flip (\(\sigma_x\)) error. We encode one logical qubit into three physical qubits via \[ |0\rangle_L = |000\rangle, \qquad |1\rangle_L = |111\rangle, \] so an arbitrary state \(|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\) is encoded as \[ |\psi\rangle_L = \alpha|000\rangle + \beta|111\rangle. \] Note that this is an entangled state of three qubits, not three copies of \(|\psi\rangle\) — no-cloning is respected.

Suppose a bit-flip occurs on the first qubit: \(\alpha|000\rangle + \beta|111\rangle \to \alpha|100\rangle + \beta|011\rangle\). To detect which qubit was flipped without disturbing the encoded information, we measure two syndrome operators: \[ Z_1 Z_2 \quad \text{and} \quad Z_2 Z_3. \] These operators check whether adjacent pairs of qubits agree, without revealing the values themselves. The syndrome outcomes are:

Error \(Z_1 Z_2\) \(Z_2 Z_3\) Correction
None \(+1\) \(+1\) \(\mathbb{I}\)
\(X_1\) \(-1\) \(+1\) \(\sigma_x^{(1)}\)
\(X_2\) \(-1\) \(-1\) \(\sigma_x^{(2)}\)
\(X_3\) \(+1\) \(-1\) \(\sigma_x^{(3)}\)

The syndrome uniquely identifies the error, and a corrective \(\sigma_x\) on the affected qubit restores the encoded state. Crucially, the syndrome measurement projects onto a subspace without revealing \(\alpha\) or \(\beta\) — it extracts information about the error while preserving the logical state.

The quantum error correction conditions

The 3-qubit code corrects only bit-flip errors. A general \([[n,k,d]]\) code must handle arbitrary errors. The fundamental result is:

Theorem (Knill–Laflamme, 1997). A code with codespace \(\mathcal{C}\) spanned by orthonormal codewords \(\{|\bar{i}\rangle\}\) can correct a set of errors \(\{E_a\}\) if and only if \[ \langle \bar{i}| E_a^\dagger E_b |\bar{j}\rangle = C_{ab}\,\delta_{ij} \] for some Hermitian matrix \(C_{ab}\) that depends on the errors but not on the codewords \(i, j\).

The \(\delta_{ij}\) condition ensures that errors do not mix different codewords (so that syndrome measurement does not disturb the logical information). The matrix \(C_{ab}\) captures the “overlap” between different errors projected onto the code space; it must be independent of which codeword was encoded.

The Steane code

A practical code must correct both bit-flip (\(\sigma_x\)) and phase-flip (\(\sigma_z\)) errors. The \([[7,1,3]]\) Steane code is the smallest code that does this. It encodes one logical qubit into seven physical qubits and has distance \(d = 3\), so it can correct any single-qubit error (including arbitrary combinations of \(\sigma_x\), \(\sigma_y\), and \(\sigma_z\)). The Steane code is a CSS code (Calderbank–Shor–Steane), meaning it is constructed from two classical codes: one correcting bit-flips and one correcting phase-flips. This modular structure makes CSS codes particularly elegant and amenable to fault-tolerant implementation.

Stabilizer formalism

The most powerful framework for describing quantum codes is the stabilizer formalism (Gottesman, 1997). A stabilizer code is defined by an abelian subgroup \(\mathcal{S}\) of the \(n\)-qubit Pauli group \(\mathcal{P}_n = \{I, X, Y, Z\}^{\otimes n}\) (up to phases) satisfying \(-\mathbb{I} \notin \mathcal{S}\). The codespace is the joint \(+1\) eigenspace of all elements of \(\mathcal{S}\): \[ \mathcal{C} = \{|\psi\rangle \in (\mathbb{C}^2)^{\otimes n} : S|\psi\rangle = +|\psi\rangle \;\; \forall \, S \in \mathcal{S}\}. \]

If \(\mathcal{S}\) is generated by \(n - k\) independent generators \(g_1, \ldots, g_{n-k}\), the codespace encodes \(k\) logical qubits. Error detection works by measuring the generators: each generator gives a \(\pm 1\) outcome, and the string of outcomes (the syndrome) identifies which error occurred without collapsing the logical state.

For the 3-qubit bit-flip code, the stabilizer group is \(\mathcal{S} = \langle Z_1Z_2,\, Z_2Z_3 \rangle\), with \(n - k = 3 - 1 = 2\) generators — precisely the syndrome operators we encountered above. For the Steane code, \(\mathcal{S}\) has \(7 - 1 = 6\) generators, three detecting \(X\) errors and three detecting \(Z\) errors.

Connection to tensor networks

The stabilizer structure of quantum codes maps naturally to tensor network representations. A stabilizer state (a state stabilized by \(2^n\) independent Pauli operators, i.e., a code with \(k = 0\)) can be represented as a tensor network whose individual tensors encode the stabilizer constraints. More generally, the encoding isometry of a quantum error-correcting code is a tensor that maps \(k\) logical indices to \(n\) physical indices — precisely the structure of a tensor network with one “bulk” layer and one “boundary” layer. This is the foundation of the holographic quantum error-correcting codes discussed in the tensor networks section below, where the bulk-to-boundary map of AdS/CFT is modeled as an isometric tensor network satisfying the quantum error correction conditions.

Connections to Tensor Networks

MPS as efficiently describable states

A generic state of \(N\) qubits lives in a \(2^N\)-dimensional Hilbert space — exponentially large, and thus intractable to describe classically. However, many physically relevant states (especially ground states of gapped local Hamiltonians in 1D) have limited entanglement and can be efficiently parameterized as matrix product states (MPS): \[ |\psi\rangle = \sum_{i_1, \ldots, i_N} \mathrm{Tr}(A^{i_1} A^{i_2} \cdots A^{i_N}) \, |i_1 i_2 \cdots i_N\rangle, \] where each \(A^{i_k}\) is a \(\chi \times \chi\) matrix. The parameter \(\chi\), the bond dimension, controls the amount of entanglement the state can carry: the entanglement entropy across any cut is bounded by \(S \leq \log \chi\).

From an information-theoretic standpoint, MPS are the natural class of states that saturate the area law for entanglement entropy in 1D: the entanglement of a contiguous block scales as the size of its boundary (which is a constant in 1D), not the volume of the block.

Area law from information theory

The area law for entanglement entropy is one of the deepest connections between quantum information and many-body physics. For ground states of gapped local Hamiltonians in \(D\) spatial dimensions, one expects \[ S(\rho_A) \sim |\partial A|, \] where \(|\partial A|\) is the area of the boundary of region \(A\). In 1D, this was rigorously proven by Hastings (2007): gapped ground states satisfy \(S \leq \text{const}\), independent of system size.

The area law explains why tensor network methods work: states with area-law entanglement occupy a tiny corner of the full Hilbert space, and tensor networks are precisely the variational class that parameterizes this corner efficiently.

Quantum error correction and holography

One of the most striking recent developments is the connection between quantum error correction, tensor networks, and holographic duality (AdS/CFT). The Ryu–Takayanagi formula relates the entanglement entropy of a boundary region \(A\) in a holographic CFT to the area of the minimal surface \(\gamma_A\) in the bulk: \[ S(\rho_A) = \frac{\mathrm{Area}(\gamma_A)}{4 G_N}. \]

This is precisely an area law, and it can be modeled by tensor networks such as the MERA (multiscale entanglement renormalization ansatz) or HaPPY code (Pastawski, Yoshida, Harlow, Preskill, 2015). In the HaPPY model, the bulk-to-boundary map is a quantum error-correcting code: bulk operators in the “entanglement wedge” of a boundary region can be reconstructed from the boundary data, even if part of the boundary is erased.

This perspective suggests that the emergence of spacetime geometry from entanglement is deeply tied to quantum error correction — a beautiful synthesis of quantum information and quantum gravity.
