Mirror Symmetry and T-Duality

Dualities that reshape our understanding of geometry

An overview of mirror symmetry and T-duality: Calabi-Yau pairs, the SYZ conjecture, and connections to string theory and brane constructions.
Author

Ajit Kumar Sorout

Published

March 23, 2026

Modified

March 24, 2026

Introduction

Mirror symmetry is one of the most profound and surprising discoveries to emerge from string theory. At its core, it asserts that two topologically distinct Calabi–Yau manifolds can give rise to identical physics when used as string compactification backgrounds. What began as a computational coincidence in the late 1980s has since grown into a deep mathematical subject, connecting enumerative geometry, symplectic topology, algebraic geometry, and homological algebra.

These notes give an overview of the key ideas: T-duality as the simplest string duality, mirror symmetry as a generalization thereof, the SYZ conjecture that connects the two, and the role of D-branes. We work at a level of detail appropriate for someone who has seen the basics of string theory and wants to understand how mirror symmetry fits together.

T-Duality: The Simplest String Duality

Circle compactification

Consider a closed bosonic string propagating on a spacetime where one direction is compactified on a circle of radius \(R\). The center-of-mass momentum along the circle is quantized: \[ p = \frac{n}{R}, \qquad n \in \mathbb{Z}, \] since the wavefunction must be single-valued, \(e^{ipX} = e^{ip(X+2\pi R)}\). But strings, unlike point particles, can also wind around the circle. The winding contribution to the energy depends on the string length stretched around the circle: \[ E_{\text{winding}} \propto \frac{w R}{\alpha'}, \qquad w \in \mathbb{Z}, \] where \(w\) is the winding number and \(\alpha' = l_s^2\) is the Regge slope.

The full mass formula for a string on \(\mathbb{R}^{1,d-1} \times S^1\) is \[ m^2 = \frac{n^2}{R^2} + \frac{w^2 R^2}{\alpha'^2} + \frac{2}{\alpha'}(N_L + N_R - 2), \] with the level-matching condition \(N_L - N_R = nw\).

The duality

The spectrum is invariant under the simultaneous exchange \[ R \;\longleftrightarrow\; \frac{\alpha'}{R}, \qquad n \;\longleftrightarrow\; w. \]

This is T-duality. A string propagating on a circle of radius \(R\) sees exactly the same physics as a string on a circle of radius \(\alpha'/R\), provided we exchange momentum modes with winding modes. In particular, the self-dual radius \(R = \sqrt{\alpha'}\) is a special point where the theory has enhanced \(SU(2)_L \times SU(2)_R\) gauge symmetry.

T-duality is not merely a classical statement: it is an exact equivalence of the full quantum string theory, order by order in the string coupling \(g_s\). It relates large and small geometries, demonstrating that string theory has a minimum meaningful length scale \(\sim \sqrt{\alpha'}\).

Detailed example: free boson on a circle

To see T-duality concretely, consider the worldsheet action for a single free boson \(X\) compactified on a circle of radius \(R\), so that \(X \sim X + 2\pi R\). The mode expansion on a closed string worldsheet (with worldsheet coordinates \(\sigma \in [0, 2\pi)\) and Euclidean time \(\tau\)) is \[ X(\tau, \sigma) = x_0 + \frac{\alpha' p}{2}\tau + w R \sigma + \frac{i}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha'}{2}} \sum_{n \neq 0} \left( \frac{\alpha_n}{n} e^{-in(\tau + \sigma)} + \frac{\tilde{\alpha}_n}{n} e^{-in(\tau - \sigma)} \right), \] where \(x_0\) is the center-of-mass position, \(p = n/R\) is the quantized momentum (\(n \in \mathbb{Z}\)), \(w \in \mathbb{Z}\) is the winding number, and \(\alpha_n\), \(\tilde{\alpha}_n\) are the left- and right-moving oscillator modes satisfying \([\alpha_m, \alpha_n] = m\,\delta_{m+n,0}\) and similarly for \(\tilde{\alpha}_n\).

It is illuminating to decompose \(X\) into left- and right-moving parts, \(X = X_L(\tau + \sigma) + X_R(\tau - \sigma)\), with \[ X_L = \frac{x_0}{2} + \frac{\alpha'}{2} p_L (\tau + \sigma) + \text{oscillators}, \qquad X_R = \frac{x_0}{2} + \frac{\alpha'}{2} p_R (\tau - \sigma) + \text{oscillators}, \] where the left- and right-moving momenta are \[ p_L = \frac{n}{R} + \frac{wR}{\alpha'}, \qquad p_R = \frac{n}{R} - \frac{wR}{\alpha'}. \] The mass-shell condition for the closed string then gives the spacetime mass \[ m^2 = p_L^2 + p_R^2 + \frac{2}{\alpha'}(N_L + N_R - 2) = \frac{n^2}{R^2} + \frac{w^2 R^2}{\alpha'^2} + \frac{2}{\alpha'}(N_L + N_R - 2), \] together with the level-matching constraint \(N_L - N_R = nw\), which follows from the requirement that \(p_L^2 - p_R^2 = 4nw/\alpha'\) be compensated by the oscillator contributions.

The key observation is that the spectrum depends on \(n\) and \(w\) only through the combination \(n^2/R^2 + w^2 R^2/\alpha'^2\), which is manifestly invariant under the simultaneous exchange \[ R \to \frac{\alpha'}{R}, \qquad n \leftrightarrow w. \] At the level of the left- and right-moving momenta, this acts as \(p_L \to p_L\), \(p_R \to -p_R\), or equivalently \(X_R \to -X_R\) while leaving \(X_L\) unchanged. This asymmetric action on the worldsheet is the hallmark of T-duality: it is a parity transformation on the right-movers only.

The partition function on the torus (the one-loop vacuum amplitude) can be written as a sum over the Narain lattice \(\Gamma_{1,1}\) of left- and right-moving momenta \((p_L, p_R)\), and T-duality is an \(O(1,1;\mathbb{Z})\) automorphism of this lattice. For compactification on \(T^d\), this generalizes to \(O(d,d;\mathbb{Z})\), the T-duality group acting on the Narain lattice \(\Gamma_{d,d}\).

T-duality and type II strings

For superstrings, T-duality has an additional twist: it exchanges the two type II theories.

  • Type IIA on \(S^1\) of radius \(R\) \(\;\longleftrightarrow\;\) Type IIB on \(S^1\) of radius \(\alpha'/R\).

The reason is that T-duality acts on the right-moving worldsheet fermions by a sign flip, which exchanges the GSO projections of IIA and IIB. This already hints at mirror symmetry: if T-duality along one circle exchanges IIA and IIB, what happens when we T-dualize along three circles simultaneously?

Calabi–Yau Geometry Primer

Before discussing mirror symmetry, we collect the essential geometric background on Calabi–Yau manifolds.

Definition and equivalent characterizations

A Calabi–Yau manifold of complex dimension \(n\) is a compact Kahler manifold \((X, g, J, \omega)\) satisfying any of the following equivalent conditions (by Yau’s theorem, proving the Calabi conjecture):

  1. Vanishing first Chern class: \(c_1(X) = 0\) in \(H^2(X, \mathbb{R})\).
  2. Ricci-flat Kahler metric: there exists a Kahler metric with \(\mathrm{Ric}(g) = 0\).
  3. \(SU(n)\) holonomy: the holonomy group of \(g\) is contained in \(SU(n)\).
  4. Trivial canonical bundle: \(K_X = \Lambda^n T^*X \cong \mathcal{O}_X\), i.e., there exists a nowhere-vanishing holomorphic \(n\)-form \(\Omega\).

The existence of the Ricci-flat metric is guaranteed by Yau’s theorem (1978): given any Kahler class \([\omega]\) on a compact Kahler manifold with \(c_1 = 0\), there exists a unique Ricci-flat Kahler metric in that class. This metric is not known explicitly in any non-trivial compact example, but its existence is sufficient for many applications.

For string theory, we primarily care about Calabi–Yau threefolds (\(n = 3\)), which provide the six extra dimensions needed to compactify ten-dimensional superstring theory to four dimensions while preserving \(\mathcal{N} = 2\) supersymmetry.

The Hodge diamond

On a compact Kahler manifold of complex dimension \(n\), the Dolbeault cohomology groups \(H^{p,q}(X)\) have dimensions \(h^{p,q} = \dim H^{p,q}(X)\), arranged in the Hodge diamond. For a Calabi–Yau threefold, the existence of \(\Omega\) and the Kahler condition impose strong constraints. The Hodge diamond simplifies to: \[ \begin{array}{ccccccc} & & & 1 & & & \\ & & 0 & & 0 & & \\ & 0 & & h^{1,1} & & 0 & \\ 1 & & h^{2,1} & & h^{2,1} & & 1 \\ & 0 & & h^{1,1} & & 0 & \\ & & 0 & & 0 & & \\ & & & 1 & & & \\ \end{array} \] Here we have used: \(h^{0,0} = h^{3,3} = 1\) (connectedness), \(h^{1,0} = h^{0,1} = 0\) (for strict \(SU(3)\) holonomy), \(h^{3,0} = h^{0,3} = 1\) (from \(\Omega\)), \(h^{2,0} = h^{0,2} = 0\), and Serre duality \(h^{p,q} = h^{n-p, n-q}\). The only independent Hodge numbers are \(h^{1,1}\) and \(h^{2,1}\).

Moduli: Kahler and complex structure

The two independent Hodge numbers have direct geometric and physical interpretations:

Kahler moduli (\(h^{1,1}\)): The Kahler form \(\omega\) is a closed real \((1,1)\)-form, and \(H^{1,1}(X, \mathbb{R})\) parametrizes the deformations of \(\omega\) within the Kahler cone. Choosing a basis \(\{e_i\}\) of \(H^{1,1}(X, \mathbb{Z})\), we write \(\omega = \sum_{i=1}^{h^{1,1}} t^i e_i\) where \(t^i > 0\) are the Kahler parameters. Physically, these control the volumes of holomorphic 2-cycles (and hence 4-cycles by Poincare duality). In string theory, these are complexified by the \(B\)-field: \(t^i_{\mathbb{C}} = \int_{C_i} (B + i\omega)\).

Complex structure moduli (\(h^{2,1}\)): By the Kodaira–Spencer theory, infinitesimal deformations of the complex structure of \(X\) are classified by \(H^1(X, TX) \cong H^{2,1}(X)\) (using the contraction with \(\Omega\) to identify \(TX \cong \Omega^2_X\)). There are \(h^{2,1}\) independent ways to deform the shape of \(X\) while preserving the Calabi–Yau condition. These deformations change the periods \(\int_{\gamma} \Omega\) of the holomorphic 3-form over 3-cycles \(\gamma \in H_3(X, \mathbb{Z})\).

The total number of massless scalar fields in the four-dimensional \(\mathcal{N} = 2\) theory is \(h^{1,1} + h^{2,1}\) (plus the dilaton and the dual of the \(B\)-field), organized into \(h^{1,1}\) vector multiplets and \(h^{2,1}\) hypermultiplets (or vice versa, depending on whether we compactify type IIA or type IIB).

Mirror Symmetry

Calabi–Yau compactification

To obtain \(\mathcal{N} = 2\) supersymmetry in four dimensions from type II strings in ten dimensions, we compactify on a Calabi–Yau threefold \(X\) — a compact Kahler manifold of complex dimension 3 with vanishing first Chern class (equivalently, with \(SU(3)\) holonomy). The topology of \(X\) determines the low-energy spectrum, and the relevant topological data is captured by the Hodge numbers \(h^{p,q}(X)\).

For a Calabi–Yau threefold, the independent Hodge numbers reduce to two: \(h^{1,1}\) and \(h^{2,1}\). These count, respectively:

  • \(h^{1,1}\): the number of Kahler moduli (deformations of the size and shape of 2-cycles),
  • \(h^{2,1}\): the number of complex structure moduli (deformations of the shape of 3-cycles).

The Euler characteristic is \(\chi = 2(h^{1,1} - h^{2,1})\).

The mirror phenomenon

Mirror symmetry asserts that for every Calabi–Yau threefold \(X\), there exists a mirror Calabi–Yau \(\widetilde{X}\) such that \[ h^{1,1}(X) = h^{2,1}(\widetilde{X}), \qquad h^{2,1}(X) = h^{1,1}(\widetilde{X}). \]

The Hodge diamond is reflected, hence the name. Since the Euler characteristics satisfy \(\chi(\widetilde{X}) = -\chi(X)\), the mirror partners are topologically distinct.

The physical content is deeper than a numerical coincidence: the full string theories are equivalent, \[ \text{Type IIA on } X \quad \longleftrightarrow \quad \text{Type IIB on } \widetilde{X}. \]

Under this duality, the Kahler moduli space of \(X\) is identified with the complex structure moduli space of \(\widetilde{X}\), and vice versa. Quantities that are difficult to compute on one side (because they receive quantum corrections) become classical computations on the mirror side.

Why mirror symmetry matters: enumerative geometry

The most celebrated early application was the computation of the number of rational curves on the quintic threefold. Consider the quintic \(X \subset \mathbb{CP}^4\), defined as the zero locus of a degree-5 polynomial. It has \(h^{1,1} = 1\) and \(h^{2,1} = 101\).

Candelas, de la Ossa, Green, and Parkes (1991) used mirror symmetry to compute the genus-zero prepotential on the Kahler moduli space of \(X\), which encodes the numbers \(n_d\) of rational curves of degree \(d\): \[ \mathcal{F} = \frac{5}{6}t^3 + \sum_{d=1}^{\infty} n_d \, \mathrm{Li}_3(e^{-dt}), \] where \(t\) is the complexified Kahler parameter and \(\mathrm{Li}_3\) is the trilogarithm. On the mirror side, \(\widetilde{X}\) has \(h^{1,1} = 101\) and \(h^{2,1} = 1\), and the prepotential is determined by the periods of the holomorphic 3-form \(\Omega\) — a classical computation involving Picard–Fuchs differential equations.

The result: \(n_1 = 2875\), \(n_2 = 609250\), \(n_3 = 317206375\), and so on. The degree-3 number had been recently computed by algebraic geometers (Ellingsrud and Stromme) and initially disagreed with the physics prediction. After a bug in the mathematicians’ code was found and fixed, the numbers agreed. This was a dramatic early confirmation of mirror symmetry.

The SYZ Conjecture

Statement

Strominger, Yau, and Zaslow (1996) proposed a geometric explanation for mirror symmetry:

SYZ Conjecture. A Calabi–Yau threefold \(X\) admitting a mirror partner \(\widetilde{X}\) has a special Lagrangian \(T^3\) fibration \[ T^3 \hookrightarrow X \xrightarrow{\pi} B, \] where \(B\) is a 3-dimensional base. The mirror \(\widetilde{X}\) is obtained by T-dualizing the \(T^3\) fibers.

A special Lagrangian submanifold \(L\) of a Calabi–Yau \(X\) is a real 3-dimensional submanifold satisfying two conditions:

  1. \(\omega|_L = 0\) (Lagrangian condition, where \(\omega\) is the Kahler form),
  2. \(\mathrm{Im}(e^{i\theta}\Omega)|_L = 0\) for some phase \(\theta\) (special condition, where \(\Omega\) is the holomorphic 3-form).

Physical reasoning

The SYZ conjecture generalizes T-duality from a single circle to three circles simultaneously. On a torus \(T^3\) of radii \(R_1, R_2, R_3\), performing T-duality on all three circles gives a dual torus with radii \(\alpha'/R_1, \alpha'/R_2, \alpha'/R_3\). Since T-duality along a single circle exchanges type IIA and IIB, T-duality along three circles (an odd number) again exchanges IIA and IIB — exactly what mirror symmetry does.

The SYZ picture is most reliable in the large complex structure limit of \(X\) (equivalently the large volume limit of \(\widetilde{X}\)), where the \(T^3\) fibers are large and the T-duality along each circle is well-defined. Away from this limit, quantum corrections modify the picture, and singular fibers (where the torus degenerates) play an important role.

Singular fibers and quantum corrections

In practice, the SYZ fibration always has singular fibers over a codimension-2 locus \(\Delta \subset B\) (the discriminant locus). Near these singular fibers, the semiclassical T-duality picture breaks down, and one must account for contributions from holomorphic discs with boundary on the Lagrangian fibers. These disc instanton corrections are responsible for much of the richness of mirror symmetry.

The discriminant locus \(\Delta\) is generically a trivalent graph (in the case of a threefold), and the singular fibers above \(\Delta\) are tori with collapsed cycles. The simplest degeneration is the Kodaira type \(I_1\) fiber, where a single 1-cycle of \(T^3\) pinches to zero size, producing a nodal torus. More complicated singularities occur at the vertices of the graph \(\Delta\), where multiple cycles degenerate simultaneously.

The physical importance of singular fibers is as follows. A D-brane (say a D2-brane in type IIA) wrapping a holomorphic disc \(D\) with boundary \(\partial D\) on a Lagrangian fiber \(L_b\) contributes a factor \(\exp(-\int_D \omega)\) to the effective action. These disc instantons correct the naive T-duality transformation in a crucial way: without them, the dual torus fibers would glue together incorrectly, and the mirror \(\widetilde{X}\) would not be a Kahler manifold.

More precisely, consider the complexified symplectic area \(A = \int_D (B + i\omega)\) of a holomorphic disc. The generating function of disc instanton contributions defines a superpotential on the moduli space of the Lagrangian fiber: \[ W(L_b) = \sum_{\beta \in \pi_2(X, L_b)} n_\beta \, e^{-A(\beta)}, \] where \(n_\beta\) is an open Gromov–Witten invariant counting discs in the class \(\beta\). These corrections are localized near the discriminant locus: far from \(\Delta\), the fibers are smooth and have no holomorphic discs ending on them (by a monotonicity argument), so the naive T-duality is valid. The non-trivial corrections arise only as one approaches the singular fibers.

The program of making the SYZ construction rigorous, incorporating these quantum corrections, has been carried out in detail for toric Calabi–Yau manifolds (by Gross and Siebert using tropical geometry, and by Auroux using symplectic techniques) and remains an active area of research for compact Calabi–Yaus. The key insight from this program is that the quantum-corrected SYZ mirror is constructed not by naive fiberwise T-duality, but by a wall-crossing procedure: as one crosses walls of marginal stability (real codimension-1 loci in \(B\) emanating from \(\Delta\)), the gluing of the dual torus fibers jumps by symplectomorphisms determined by the disc counts.

The Quintic and Its Mirror

The quintic threefold

The quintic threefold is the hypersurface \[ X_5 = \left\{ [z_0 : z_1 : z_2 : z_3 : z_4] \in \mathbb{CP}^4 \;\middle|\; z_0^5 + z_1^5 + z_2^5 + z_3^5 + z_4^5 + \text{lower-order terms} = 0 \right\}, \] defined as the zero locus of a generic homogeneous polynomial of degree 5 in the five homogeneous coordinates of \(\mathbb{CP}^4\). By the Lefschetz hyperplane theorem and adjunction, \(X_5\) is a smooth Calabi–Yau threefold: the canonical bundle is \(K_{X_5} = \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{CP}^4}(-5)|_{X_5} \otimes \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{CP}^4}(5)|_{X_5} = \mathcal{O}_{X_5}\), confirming \(c_1 = 0\).

The Hodge numbers are computed from the Lefschetz theorem and residue calculus: \[ h^{1,1}(X_5) = 1, \qquad h^{2,1}(X_5) = 101. \] The single Kahler modulus corresponds to the overall size of the quintic (inherited from the hyperplane class of \(\mathbb{CP}^4\)). The 101 complex structure moduli parametrize the choice of degree-5 polynomial: the space of such polynomials has dimension \(\binom{5+4}{4} = 126\), modulo the \(\dim PGL(5) = 24\) coordinate reparametrizations, giving \(126 - 24 - 1 = 101\) independent deformations (the \(-1\) accounts for overall rescaling). The Euler characteristic is \(\chi = 2(1 - 101) = -200\).

The Greene–Plesser mirror construction

The mirror of the quintic was constructed by Greene and Plesser (1990) using an orbifold procedure. Consider the special one-parameter subfamily (the Fermat pencil) \[ X_\psi: \quad z_0^5 + z_1^5 + z_2^5 + z_3^5 + z_4^5 - 5\psi \, z_0 z_1 z_2 z_3 z_4 = 0, \] and define the group \(G = (\mathbb{Z}_5)^3\), realized as the subgroup of \((\mathbb{Z}_5)^4\) acting by \[ (z_0, z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4) \mapsto (\omega^{a_1} z_0, \omega^{a_2} z_1, \omega^{a_3} z_2, \omega^{a_4} z_3, z_4), \qquad \omega = e^{2\pi i/5}, \] where \((a_1, a_2, a_3, a_4) \in (\mathbb{Z}_5)^4\) with \(a_1 + a_2 + a_3 + a_4 \equiv 0 \pmod{5}\) (this condition ensures the polynomial \(X_\psi\) is invariant), modulo the overall diagonal action \((1,1,1,1,1)\) which acts trivially on \(\mathbb{CP}^4\). The mirror quintic is obtained as the crepant resolution of the quotient: \[ \widetilde{X}_5 = \widehat{X_\psi / G}. \] The orbifold action collapses most of the complex structure moduli but introduces new Kahler moduli from the resolution of singularities. The result has Hodge numbers \[ h^{1,1}(\widetilde{X}_5) = 101, \qquad h^{2,1}(\widetilde{X}_5) = 1, \] confirming the mirror exchange \(h^{1,1} \leftrightarrow h^{2,1}\).

Periods and the Picard–Fuchs equation

The single complex structure parameter \(\psi\) of \(\widetilde{X}_5\) maps to the complexified Kahler parameter of \(X_5\). The holomorphic 3-form \(\Omega(\psi)\) on \(\widetilde{X}_5\) has periods \(\Pi_i(\psi) = \int_{\gamma_i} \Omega\) over a symplectic basis \(\{\gamma_i\}\) of \(H_3(\widetilde{X}_5, \mathbb{Z})\). These periods satisfy a fourth-order Picard–Fuchs differential equation in the variable \(z = (5\psi)^{-5}\): \[ \left[ \theta^4 - 5z(5\theta + 1)(5\theta + 2)(5\theta + 3)(5\theta + 4) \right] \Pi(z) = 0, \qquad \theta = z\frac{d}{dz}. \] Near the large complex structure point \(z = 0\) (equivalently \(\psi \to \infty\)), the four independent solutions have the structure \(\Pi_0 = 1 + \mathcal{O}(z)\), \(\Pi_1 = \Pi_0 \log z + \mathcal{O}(z)\), and two further solutions involving \((\log z)^2\) and \((\log z)^3\). The mirror map \(t(z) = \Pi_1/\Pi_0\) identifies the flat coordinate \(t\) on the Kahler moduli space of \(X_5\), and the prepotential \(\mathcal{F}(t)\) is extracted from the ratios of periods.

Mirror Symmetry and Enumerative Geometry

The deepest computational power of mirror symmetry lies in the relationship between the A-model on \(X\) and the B-model on \(\widetilde{X}\). These are two topologically twisted versions of the \(\mathcal{N} = (2,2)\) worldsheet sigma model, each capturing different geometric information.

The A-model: Gromov–Witten invariants

The A-model on a Calabi–Yau \(X\) depends only on the Kahler (symplectic) structure of \(X\), and its observables are the Gromov–Witten invariants. At genus zero, the relevant objects are holomorphic maps \(f: \mathbb{CP}^1 \to X\) whose image represents a class \(\beta \in H_2(X, \mathbb{Z})\). The genus-zero Gromov–Witten invariant \(N_\beta\) (roughly) counts the number of such maps, weighted by signs from the obstruction theory.

For the quintic with \(h^{1,1} = 1\), the class \(\beta\) is determined by the degree \(d = \int_\beta \omega\), and the A-model genus-zero free energy takes the form \[ \mathcal{F}_A(t) = \frac{5}{6}t^3 + \sum_{d=1}^{\infty} N_d \, q^d, \qquad q = e^{2\pi i t}, \] where \(t\) is the complexified Kahler parameter and \(N_d\) is the genus-zero Gromov–Witten invariant at degree \(d\). The cubic term \(\frac{5}{6}t^3\) is the classical triple intersection number \(\int_X \omega^3 = 5\) (divided by \(3! = 6\)). The instanton sum \(\sum N_d q^d\) encodes the quantum corrections from holomorphic curves.

The relation between the Gromov–Witten invariants \(N_d\) and the “BPS numbers” \(n_d\) (the rational curve counts appearing in the prepotential formula with the trilogarithm) is given by the multi-cover formula: \[ N_d = \sum_{k | d} n_{d/k} \frac{1}{k^3}. \] The integers \(n_d\) are the genuinely enumerative quantities, counting (in an appropriate sense) the number of isolated rational curves of degree \(d\).

The B-model: periods and variation of Hodge structure

The B-model on a Calabi–Yau \(\widetilde{X}\) depends only on the complex structure of \(\widetilde{X}\), and its observables are governed by the variation of Hodge structure (VHS) on the family of Calabi–Yau manifolds as the complex structure moduli vary. The central object is the holomorphic 3-form \(\Omega\) and its periods \(\Pi_i = \int_{\gamma_i} \Omega\).

As the complex structure moduli vary, \(\Omega\) traces out a path in the Hodge filtration \(F^3 \subset F^2 \subset F^1 \subset H^3(\widetilde{X}, \mathbb{C})\), and the B-model free energy is determined by the special geometry relations among the periods. The crucial point is that B-model computations are exact at tree level — there are no worldsheet instanton corrections on the B-model side. This is because the B-model path integral localizes on constant maps, and the moduli space of constant maps is just \(\widetilde{X}\) itself.

The mirror map and computational power

Mirror symmetry equates the A-model on \(X\) with the B-model on \(\widetilde{X}\): \[ \mathcal{F}_A^{X}(t) = \mathcal{F}_B^{\widetilde{X}}(\psi(t)). \] The left side involves an infinite sum over worldsheet instantons (holomorphic curves), each of which is extremely difficult to compute directly on \(X\). The right side is a classical period computation on \(\widetilde{X}\), involving only ordinary differential equations (the Picard–Fuchs system). The mirror map \(t(\psi)\) relates the natural coordinates on the two sides.

This is the computational miracle: an infinite series of enumerative invariants, each requiring sophisticated algebraic geometry or symplectic topology to compute individually, is encoded in the solution of an ODE. For the quintic, this yields \(n_1 = 2875\) (the classical result of Schubert calculus), \(n_2 = 609{,}250\), \(n_3 = 317{,}206{,}375\), \(n_4 = 242{,}467{,}530{,}000\), and so on to arbitrarily high degree.

D-Branes and Mirror Symmetry

Branes wrapping cycles

D-branes are extended objects on which open strings can end. A D\(p\)-brane in type II string theory wraps a \((p+1)\)-dimensional worldvolume. In a Calabi–Yau compactification:

  • Type IIA has D0, D2, D4, D6-branes wrapping even-dimensional cycles of \(X\).
  • Type IIB has D(-1), D1, D3, D5, D7-branes wrapping odd-dimensional cycles of \(X\).

Mirror symmetry exchanges the even and odd cycles. More precisely, it exchanges the A-branes (Lagrangian submanifolds, natural for type IIA) with B-branes (holomorphic submanifolds, natural for type IIB). In the SYZ picture, a D0-brane (a point) on \(X\) dualizes to a D3-brane wrapping the full \(T^3\) fiber on \(\widetilde{X}\), which is a special Lagrangian 3-cycle.

Calibrated geometry

The conditions for a D-brane to preserve supersymmetry are captured by calibrated geometry. A \(p\)-form \(\phi\) on a Riemannian manifold is a calibration if \(d\phi = 0\) and \(\phi|_{\Sigma} \leq \mathrm{vol}_\Sigma\) for every oriented \(p\)-dimensional submanifold \(\Sigma\). A submanifold saturating the bound is calibrated and minimizes volume in its homology class.

On a Calabi–Yau threefold, the relevant calibrations are:

  • \(\omega\) (the Kahler form) for holomorphic curves,
  • \(\frac{1}{2}\omega^2\) for holomorphic surfaces,
  • \(\mathrm{Re}(\Omega)\) for special Lagrangian 3-cycles.

Mirror symmetry exchanges the Kahler calibration data with the complex structure calibration data.

Connections to Brane Tilings

For toric Calabi–Yau geometries — non-compact Calabi–Yau threefolds described by toric diagrams — mirror symmetry has a particularly explicit realization. The mirror of a toric Calabi–Yau singularity \(X\) can be written as a hypersurface \[ uv = W(x, y) \] in \(\mathbb{C}^2 \times (\mathbb{C}^*)^2\), where the curve \(W(x,y) = 0\) is the mirror curve (or spectral curve). The toric data of \(X\) is encoded in the Newton polygon of \(W\).

From toric data to mirror curves

A toric Calabi–Yau threefold singularity is specified by a convex lattice polygon \(\Delta \subset \mathbb{Z}^2\) (the toric diagram). The mirror curve is a Riemann surface \(\Sigma\) embedded in \((\mathbb{C}^*)^2\), defined by a Laurent polynomial \[ W(x,y) = \sum_{(m,n) \in \Delta \cap \mathbb{Z}^2} a_{m,n} \, x^m y^n = 0, \] whose Newton polygon is precisely \(\Delta\). The coefficients \(a_{m,n}\) encode the complex structure moduli of the mirror, which by mirror symmetry correspond to the Kahler moduli (resolution parameters and \(B\)-field periods) of \(X\). The genus of the mirror curve \(\Sigma\) equals the number of interior lattice points of \(\Delta\), which counts the number of compact 4-cycles in \(X\).

Brane tilings and the Kasteleyn matrix

Brane tilings (also called dimer models) provide a combinatorial bridge: a periodic bipartite graph \(\mathcal{G}\) on \(T^2\) encodes both the quiver gauge theory on D3-branes probing the toric singularity \(X\), and the geometry of the mirror. The faces of \(\mathcal{G}\) correspond to gauge group factors (nodes of the quiver), edges to bifundamental fields, and the bipartite structure determines the superpotential.

The connection to the mirror curve proceeds through the Kasteleyn matrix \(K(x,y)\). This is the weighted adjacency matrix of the bipartite graph, where each edge \(e\) connecting a black vertex \(b\) to a white vertex \(w\) receives a weight that includes reference fugacities \(x^{n_1(e)} y^{n_2(e)}\) tracking the homology class of the edge on \(T^2\). The characteristic polynomial of the Kasteleyn matrix, \[ P(x, y) = \det K(x, y), \] is a Laurent polynomial in \(x\) and \(y\) whose zero locus \(P(x,y) = 0\) defines the mirror curve \(\Sigma\). The Newton polygon of \(P\) recovers the toric diagram \(\Delta\) of \(X\).

This gives a concrete and computational pipeline: starting from a brane tiling (which can be constructed systematically for any toric singularity), one reads off the Kasteleyn matrix, computes its determinant, and obtains the mirror curve together with its embedding in \((\mathbb{C}^*)^2\). The zig-zag paths of the tiling — oriented paths that turn maximally left at white vertices and maximally right at black vertices — are in bijection with the external legs of the toric diagram, providing a direct combinatorial map between the tiling and the toric geometry.

Example: the conifold

For the simplest non-trivial example, the conifold \(\mathcal{O}(-1) \oplus \mathcal{O}(-1) \to \mathbb{CP}^1\), the toric diagram is a line segment with two interior points, and the brane tiling is a square lattice on \(T^2\) with two faces. The Kasteleyn matrix is \(2 \times 2\): \[ K(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + x \\ 1 + y \end{pmatrix}^T, \] giving the mirror curve \(P(x,y) = 1 + x + y + xy = (1+x)(1+y)\). After a deformation by a complex structure parameter \(z\), this becomes \(P(x,y) = 1 + x + y + z\,xy\), a genus-0 curve whose periods encode the resolved conifold geometry.

Homological Mirror Symmetry

In 1994, Kontsevich proposed a far-reaching mathematical formulation of mirror symmetry:

Kontsevich’s Homological Mirror Symmetry (HMS) Conjecture. For a mirror pair \((X, \widetilde{X})\) of Calabi–Yau manifolds, \[ D^b\mathrm{Coh}(X) \simeq D^\pi\mathrm{Fuk}(\widetilde{X}), \] where \(D^b\mathrm{Coh}(X)\) is the bounded derived category of coherent sheaves on \(X\) (encoding B-branes), and \(D^\pi\mathrm{Fuk}(\widetilde{X})\) is the derived Fukaya category of \(\widetilde{X}\) (encoding A-branes, i.e., Lagrangian submanifolds with flat \(U(1)\) connections).

HMS elevates mirror symmetry from a statement about numbers (Hodge numbers, curve counts) to an equivalence of categorical structures. It has been proven in a number of cases, including elliptic curves (Polishchuk and Zaslow), the quartic K3 surface (Seidel), and toric varieties (various authors). The general case for compact Calabi–Yau threefolds remains open.

From the physics perspective, HMS is the statement that the open string sectors (D-brane categories) of the two mirror theories are equivalent, which is a natural consequence of the underlying string duality.
