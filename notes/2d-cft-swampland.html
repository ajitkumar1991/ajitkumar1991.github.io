2D CFT, Orbifolding, and the Swampland

Conformal symmetry, discrete gauging, and the boundaries of the landscape

Two-dimensional conformal field theory, orbifold constructions, zeta regularization, and the swampland program — with connections to string compactifications.
Author

Ajit Kumar Sorout

Published

November 15, 2025

Modified

March 24, 2026

Introduction

Two-dimensional conformal field theory sits at the heart of string theory. The worldsheet theory of a string propagating in spacetime is a 2D CFT, and the consistency conditions of this CFT — modular invariance, unitarity, the correct operator content — determine the allowed spacetime physics. Understanding 2D CFT is therefore not optional for anyone working on string compactifications.

These notes cover the essential machinery: the Virasoro algebra and its representation theory, the state-operator correspondence, modular invariance of the torus partition function, and orbifold constructions. We also include a detailed treatment of zeta regularization (explaining the origin of the \(-c/24\) shift and the critical dimension \(D=26\)), and close with the swampland program, which uses string consistency conditions to constrain effective field theories coupled to gravity.

The Conformal Group in Two Dimensions

Why 2D is special

In \(d\) dimensions, the conformal group is finite-dimensional: it is \(SO(d+1,1)\) in Euclidean signature, with \(\frac{1}{2}(d+1)(d+2)\) generators. But in \(d=2\), something remarkable happens. The conformal transformations are the holomorphic and antiholomorphic maps: \[ z \mapsto f(z), \qquad \bar{z} \mapsto \bar{f}(\bar{z}), \] where \(f\) and \(\bar{f}\) are (locally) arbitrary holomorphic functions. The algebra of infinitesimal conformal transformations is therefore infinite-dimensional, generated by \[ \ell_n = -z^{n+1}\partial_z, \qquad \bar{\ell}_n = -\bar{z}^{n+1}\partial_{\bar{z}}, \qquad n \in \mathbb{Z}. \]

These satisfy \[ [\ell_m, \ell_n] = (m-n)\ell_{m+n}, \qquad [\bar{\ell}_m, \bar{\ell}_n] = (m-n)\bar{\ell}_{m+n}, \qquad [\ell_m, \bar{\ell}_n] = 0. \]

This is two copies of the Witt algebra. The global conformal group — the subgroup that is well-defined on the Riemann sphere \(\mathbb{CP}^1\) — is generated by \(\{\ell_{-1}, \ell_0, \ell_1\}\) and their antiholomorphic counterparts, giving \(SL(2,\mathbb{C}) / \mathbb{Z}_2\), the group of Mobius transformations.

The Virasoro algebra

In the quantum theory, the Witt algebra acquires a central extension. The generators \(L_n\) of the holomorphic Virasoro algebra satisfy \[ [L_m, L_n] = (m-n)L_{m+n} + \frac{c}{12}m(m^2-1)\delta_{m+n,0}, \] where \(c\) is the central charge. The antiholomorphic copy has generators \(\bar{L}_n\) with central charge \(\bar{c}\) (often \(\bar{c} = c\) for non-chiral theories). The central extension is unique up to normalization: \(H^2(\text{Witt}, \mathbb{C}) = \mathbb{C}\).

The central charge is the most important datum of a 2D CFT. It counts the “number of degrees of freedom” in a rough sense: a free boson has \(c = 1\), a free Majorana fermion has \(c = 1/2\), and a system of \(n\) free bosons has \(c = n\). The unitarity bound requires \(c \geq 0\) (for the discrete series, \(c\) takes the values \(1 - 6/m(m+1)\) for \(m = 3, 4, 5, \ldots\), approaching \(c = 1\) from below).

Primary operators and the state-operator correspondence

A primary operator \(\mathcal{O}(z, \bar{z})\) of conformal weights \((h, \bar{h})\) transforms under conformal transformations as \[ \mathcal{O}(z, \bar{z}) \mapsto \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right)^h \left(\frac{\partial \bar{f}}{\partial \bar{z}}\right)^{\bar{h}} \mathcal{O}(f(z), \bar{f}(\bar{z})). \]

Equivalently, the Virasoro generators act on the state \(|h\rangle = \mathcal{O}(0)|0\rangle\) as \[ L_0 |h\rangle = h|h\rangle, \qquad L_n |h\rangle = 0 \quad \text{for } n > 0. \]

The state-operator correspondence is the bijection between local operators and states in radial quantization: inserting \(\mathcal{O}(0)\) at the origin of the complex plane creates the state \(|h, \bar{h}\rangle\) on the unit circle. This is a powerful organizing principle that has no direct analogue in higher-dimensional QFT.

The full Hilbert space is built by acting with the creation operators \(L_{-n}\) (\(n > 0\)) on primary states. The resulting states \(L_{-n_1} L_{-n_2} \cdots |h\rangle\) are called descendants, and the primary together with all its descendants forms a Verma module \(V_{c,h}\).

The operator product expansion

The OPE of two primary operators takes the form \[ \mathcal{O}_i(z) \mathcal{O}_j(0) = \sum_k C_{ij}^{\;\;k} \, z^{h_k - h_i - h_j} \bigl[\mathcal{O}_k(0) + \text{descendants}\bigr], \] where \(C_{ij}^{\;\;k}\) are the OPE coefficients (or structure constants). The OPE is an operator identity, valid inside any correlation function, and it encodes the entire dynamical content of the theory. Associativity of the OPE imposes powerful constraints — the bootstrap equations — that in favorable cases determine the theory completely.

Modular Invariance

The torus partition function

Placing a 2D CFT on a torus \(T^2\) with modular parameter \(\tau\) (with \(\mathrm{Im}(\tau) > 0\)), the partition function is \[ Z(\tau, \bar{\tau}) = \mathrm{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}}\bigl(q^{L_0 - c/24} \, \bar{q}^{\bar{L}_0 - \bar{c}/24}\bigr), \qquad q = e^{2\pi i \tau}. \]

The shifts \(-c/24\) and \(-\bar{c}/24\) arise from the conformal anomaly when mapping from the plane to the torus (see the zeta regularization section below for the physical origin).

The torus is defined by identifications \(z \sim z + 1 \sim z + \tau\), and different values of \(\tau\) related by \(SL(2,\mathbb{Z})\) transformations describe the same torus. The modular group \(SL(2,\mathbb{Z})\) is generated by \[ T: \tau \mapsto \tau + 1, \qquad S: \tau \mapsto -1/\tau. \]

Physical consistency of the CFT on the torus requires modular invariance: \[ Z(\tau + 1, \bar{\tau} + 1) = Z(\tau, \bar{\tau}), \qquad Z(-1/\tau, -1/\bar{\tau}) = Z(\tau, \bar{\tau}). \]

Modular S and T matrices

For a rational CFT (RCFT) with a finite number of primary fields \(\phi_i\), \(i = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1\), the partition function decomposes as \[ Z(\tau, \bar{\tau}) = \sum_{i,j} M_{ij} \, \chi_i(\tau) \, \overline{\chi_j(\tau)}, \] where \(\chi_i(\tau) = \mathrm{Tr}_{V_i}(q^{L_0 - c/24})\) is the character of the Verma module \(V_i\), and \(M_{ij}\) is a matrix of non-negative integers with \(M_{00} = 1\) (uniqueness of the vacuum).

Under the modular transformations, the characters transform linearly: \[ \chi_i(\tau + 1) = \sum_j T_{ij} \chi_j(\tau), \qquad \chi_i(-1/\tau) = \sum_j S_{ij} \chi_j(\tau), \] where \(T_{ij} = e^{2\pi i (h_i - c/24)} \delta_{ij}\) and \(S_{ij}\) is the modular S-matrix, a unitary symmetric matrix.

Modular invariance then requires \([M, S] = [M, T] = 0\). Classifying all such \(M\) is the modular invariant classification problem. For \(SU(2)\) WZW models, this was solved by Cappelli, Itzykson, and Zuber, yielding the famous ADE classification: the modular invariants are in correspondence with the simply-laced Lie algebras \(A_n\), \(D_n\), \(E_6\), \(E_7\), \(E_8\).

The Verlinde formula

The modular S-matrix also determines the fusion rules via the Verlinde formula: \[ N_{ij}^{\;\;k} = \sum_l \frac{S_{il} S_{jl} S^*_{kl}}{S_{0l}}, \] where \(N_{ij}^{\;\;k}\) are the fusion coefficients appearing in the OPE \(\phi_i \times \phi_j = \sum_k N_{ij}^{\;\;k} \phi_k\). That the modular S-matrix (a property of the torus) determines the OPE (a property of the sphere) is a deep consequence of the consistency of CFT on all Riemann surfaces.

Example: The Free Boson Partition Function

The free boson compactified on a circle of radius \(R\) is the canonical example for seeing modular invariance at work. The partition function is: \[ Z(\tau) = \frac{1}{|\eta(\tau)|^2} \sum_{n, w \in \mathbb{Z}} q^{\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{n}{R} + wR\right)^2} \, \bar{q}^{\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{n}{R} - wR\right)^2} \] where \(n\) is the momentum quantum number, \(w\) is the winding number, \(q = e^{2\pi i \tau}\), and \(\eta(\tau) = q^{1/24} \prod_{n=1}^\infty (1 - q^n)\) is the Dedekind eta function.

The sum over \(n\) and \(w\) is a lattice sum over the Narain lattice \(\Gamma_{1,1}\), a two-dimensional even self-dual lattice with signature \((1,1)\). The key to modular invariance is that:

  • Under \(T: \tau \to \tau + 1\), the lattice sum picks up phases \(e^{2\pi i (h - \bar{h})}\) which are integers by the evenness of \(\Gamma_{1,1}\).
  • Under \(S: \tau \to -1/\tau\), Poisson resummation exchanges momentum and winding, mapping \(\Gamma_{1,1} \to \Gamma_{1,1}^*\). Self-duality of the Narain lattice ensures \(\Gamma_{1,1}^* = \Gamma_{1,1}\), so the partition function is invariant.

This is the pattern for all toroidal compactifications: modular invariance of the partition function is equivalent to the Narain lattice being even and self-dual. For compactification on \(T^d\), the lattice is \(\Gamma_{d,d}\), and the moduli space of even self-dual lattices of signature \((d,d)\) is \(O(d,d;\mathbb{Z}) \backslash O(d,d;\mathbb{R}) / (O(d) \times O(d))\). This is the moduli space of Narain CFTs.

The Minimal Models

The Virasoro minimal models \(\mathcal{M}(m, m+1)\) for \(m = 3, 4, 5, \ldots\) are the simplest examples of interacting 2D CFTs. They have central charge: \[ c = 1 - \frac{6}{m(m+1)} \] and a finite number of primary fields \(\phi_{r,s}\) with conformal weights: \[ h_{r,s} = \frac{[(m+1)r - ms]^2 - 1}{4m(m+1)}, \qquad 1 \leq r \leq m-1, \quad 1 \leq s \leq m. \]

The first few examples are:

\(m\) \(c\) Theory Primary fields
3 \(1/2\) Ising model \(\mathbb{1}\), \(\sigma\) (\(h = 1/16\)), \(\varepsilon\) (\(h = 1/2\))
4 \(7/10\) Tricritical Ising 6 primaries, including \(\sigma\) (\(h = 3/80\)) and \(\varepsilon\) (\(h = 1/10\))
5 \(4/5\) Three-state Potts 10 primaries

The modular invariant partition function for each minimal model has been classified (ADE classification): the diagonal invariant \(Z = \sum_i |\chi_i|^2\) always works, but non-diagonal invariants exist and correspond to the D and E series of simply-laced Lie algebras.

The minimal models are exactly the CFTs that appear at the critical points of 2D lattice models: the Ising model at \(c = 1/2\), the tricritical Ising at \(c = 7/10\) (which appears in my work on boundary RG flows in Majorana chains), and so on. Their finite operator content makes them exactly solvable: all correlation functions can be computed from the OPE coefficients and conformal blocks.

Zeta Regularization, Vacuum Energy, and the Critical Dimension

The Riemann zeta function and analytic continuation

The Riemann zeta function is defined for \(\mathrm{Re}(s) > 1\) by the convergent series \[ \zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^s}. \]

For \(\mathrm{Re}(s) \leq 1\) this series diverges, but \(\zeta(s)\) has an analytic continuation to a meromorphic function on the entire complex plane, with a single simple pole at \(s = 1\). The functional equation \[ \zeta(s) = 2^s \pi^{s-1} \sin\!\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right) \Gamma(1-s) \, \zeta(1-s) \] relates values at \(s\) and \(1-s\).

In particular, at \(s = -1\) the analytic continuation gives \[ \zeta(-1) = -\frac{1}{12}. \]

Formally, one writes \[ \zeta(-1) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n \quad \text{(zeta-regularized)}. \]

This does not mean the ordinary sum \(1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots\) converges — it diverges to \(+\infty\) in every conventional sense. But in QFT, CFT, and string theory, we frequently encounter divergent sums of this type from zero-point energies, and replacing them by their zeta-regularized values is a consistent and physically correct procedure: \[ 1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots \;\xrightarrow{\text{zeta reg.}}\; \zeta(-1) = -\frac{1}{12}. \]

Ground-state energy in 2D CFT and the \(-c/24\) shift

Consider a 2D CFT on a circle of circumference \(L\). The Hamiltonian on the cylinder is related to the Virasoro generators by \[ H = \frac{2\pi}{L}\left(L_0 + \bar{L}_0 - \frac{c}{12}\right). \]

The vacuum is not at zero energy; instead, \[ E_0 = -\frac{\pi c}{6L}. \]

This Casimir energy arises from normal ordering the infinite sum of oscillator zero-point energies. For a single chiral boson, the modes are labeled by positive integers \(n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\) and each mode contributes zero-point energy \(\frac{1}{2}\omega_n\) with \(\omega_n \propto n\) on the circle: \[ E_{\text{zero-point}} \propto \frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n. \]

This sum diverges, but zeta regularization gives \[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n \;\xrightarrow{\text{zeta reg.}}\; \zeta(-1) = -\frac{1}{12}, \] yielding a finite negative vacuum energy. This is the origin of the shifts \[ L_0 \to L_0 - \frac{c}{24}, \] and the factor \(q^{-c/24}\) in the torus partition function \[ Z(\tau) = \mathrm{Tr}\bigl(q^{L_0 - c/24} \, \bar{q}^{\bar{L}_0 - \bar{c}/24}\bigr), \qquad q = e^{2\pi i \tau}. \]

The shift \(-c/24\) is not merely a regularization artifact: it is required by modular invariance. The partition function \(Z(\tau)\) must be invariant under \(\tau \to \tau + 1\), which requires the spectrum of \(L_0\) to differ from integers by \(c/24 \pmod{1}\). The zeta regularization is the mechanism that implements this physical requirement.

The bosonic string and the critical dimension \(D = 26\)

In the bosonic string, we have \(D\) spacetime coordinates \(X^\mu\). In light-cone gauge, only \(D-2\) transverse coordinates are physical oscillators. For each transverse boson, the normal-ordering constant is \[ a_{\text{1 boson}} = -\frac{1}{24}, \] coming from \(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n \to \frac{1}{2}\zeta(-1) = -\frac{1}{24}\).

For \(D-2\) transverse bosons, the total intercept is \[ a = -\frac{D-2}{24}. \]

The mass formula for an open bosonic string (in units with \(\alpha' = 1\)) is \[ m^2 = N - a, \] where \(N\) is the level number. The first excited state (\(N = 1\)) is a vector, and for the string spectrum to be consistent (specifically, for this vector to be massless and form a representation of the little group \(SO(D-2)\)), we require \[ a = 1 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \frac{D-2}{24} = 1 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad D = 26. \]

The critical dimension of the bosonic string is \(D = 26\), and the value \(-1/12\) from \(\zeta(-1)\) is the key ingredient. For the superstring, the additional fermionic oscillators modify the calculation and give \(D = 10\).

Orbifolds

Gauging a discrete symmetry

An orbifold is obtained by gauging a discrete symmetry group \(G\) of a CFT. If the parent theory has a symmetry \(G\) acting on the fields, the orbifold theory \(\mathcal{C}/G\) is constructed in two steps:

  1. Project onto \(G\)-invariant states in the original (untwisted) Hilbert space.
  2. Add twisted sectors: for each conjugacy class \([g]\) of \(G\), include a new sector \(\mathcal{H}_g\) of states satisfying twisted boundary conditions.

Both steps are necessary for modular invariance. The projection alone would break modular invariance (the \(S\)-transform of an untwisted, projected state involves twisted states), and the twisted sectors restore it.

The orbifold partition function

For an abelian group \(G\), the orbifold partition function on the torus is \[ Z_{\text{orb}}(\tau) = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g, h \in G \atop [g,h]=e} Z_{g,h}(\tau), \] where \(Z_{g,h}(\tau) = \mathrm{Tr}_{\mathcal{H}_g}\bigl(h \cdot q^{L_0 - c/24} \bar{q}^{\bar{L}_0 - \bar{c}/24}\bigr)\) is the trace over the \(g\)-twisted sector with an insertion of \(h\) (implementing the projection). The sum runs over commuting pairs \((g,h)\) because the torus has two independent cycles, and a consistent assignment of boundary conditions requires the monodromies around the two cycles to commute.

Under the modular \(S\)-transformation \(\tau \to -1/\tau\), the two cycles of the torus are exchanged, so \(Z_{g,h} \to Z_{h,g^{-1}}\). Under \(T: \tau \to \tau+1\), we get \(Z_{g,h} \to Z_{g, gh}\). The sum over all commuting pairs is manifestly invariant under both.

Canonical example: the \(\mathbb{Z}_2\) orbifold of the free boson

Consider a free boson \(X\) compactified on a circle of radius \(R\), with the \(\mathbb{Z}_2\) symmetry \(X \to -X\). The orbifold \(S^1/\mathbb{Z}_2\) is a line segment (an interval), and the resulting CFT is the theory on this interval with appropriate boundary conditions at the endpoints.

Untwisted sector: We keep only the \(\mathbb{Z}_2\)-even states of the original circle theory. This eliminates states with odd winding or momentum quantum numbers from the original partition function.

Twisted sector: The \(\mathbb{Z}_2\)-twisted boundary condition is \(X(\sigma + 2\pi) = -X(\sigma)\), so the mode expansion has half-integer moding: \(\alpha_{n+1/2}\) instead of \(\alpha_n\). There is no zero mode (the string is stuck at a fixed point), and the ground-state energy shifts to \[ E_0^{\text{tw}} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right) \;\xrightarrow{\text{zeta reg.}}\; \frac{1}{2}\zeta(-1, 1/2) = \frac{1}{48}, \] where \(\zeta(s, a) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+a)^{-s}\) is the Hurwitz zeta function.

This orbifold produces two fixed points on the circle (at \(X = 0\) and \(X = \pi R\)), each contributing a twisted-sector ground state. The full partition function combines untwisted and twisted sectors and is modular invariant.

The \(\mathbb{Z}_2\) orbifold of the free boson is the simplest example of an orbifold construction, but the technique generalizes enormously. The \(\mathbb{Z}_5\) orbifold used by Greene and Plesser to construct the mirror of the quintic is a direct higher-dimensional analogue.

Discrete Torsion

Definition and classification

When orbifolding by a non-abelian group \(G\) (or even an abelian group with multiple generators), there is an additional choice: discrete torsion, introduced by Vafa (1986).

Different consistent orbifold theories associated to the same group \(G\) are classified by the group cohomology \[ H^2(G, U(1)). \]

An element \(\epsilon \in H^2(G, U(1))\) is a \(U(1)\)-valued 2-cocycle \(\epsilon(g, h)\) satisfying the cocycle condition, modulo coboundaries. In the orbifold partition function, discrete torsion modifies the phases: \[ Z_{\text{orb}}^{(\epsilon)} = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g,h \atop [g,h]=e} \epsilon(g,h) \, Z_{g,h}(\tau). \]

Physical effects

Discrete torsion changes the relative signs between twisted sectors, and thus modifies the spectrum of the orbifold theory. Two orbifolds related by different choices of discrete torsion have the same untwisted sector but different twisted sectors. In the context of Calabi–Yau orbifolds, discrete torsion can change the Hodge numbers of the resulting manifold.

For the simplest non-trivial example, \(G = \mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2\), we have \(H^2(\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2, U(1)) = \mathbb{Z}_2\), giving two inequivalent orbifold theories (with and without discrete torsion).

The Swampland Program

The landscape and the swampland

String theory has an enormous number of vacua — the string landscape, estimated at \(10^{500}\) or more. But not every low-energy effective field theory (EFT) coupled to gravity can arise from a consistent theory of quantum gravity. The set of consistent EFTs is the landscape; the set of apparently consistent but actually inconsistent EFTs is the swampland.

The swampland program, initiated by Vafa (2005), aims to identify general principles that separate the landscape from the swampland. These principles take the form of swampland conjectures — proposed necessary conditions for an EFT to admit a UV completion in quantum gravity.

Key swampland conjectures

No global symmetries. Every continuous global symmetry in a theory of quantum gravity must be either gauged or broken. This is supported by black hole arguments (a black hole can swallow global charge, and when it evaporates, the charge is lost, violating conservation) and by explicit string constructions (where every symmetry has a gauge origin).

Completeness hypothesis. For every gauge symmetry, all representations allowed by Dirac quantization must appear in the spectrum. In string theory, this is typically realized through the existence of appropriate branes.

Weak gravity conjecture (WGC). For a \(U(1)\) gauge theory with coupling \(g\) coupled to gravity, there must exist a particle of charge \(q\) and mass \(m\) satisfying \[ m \leq \sqrt{2}\, g \, q \, M_{\rm Pl}, \] (in 4D, with appropriate conventions). In other words, gravity must be the weakest force. The WGC has many generalizations (to \(p\)-form gauge fields, to multiple \(U(1)\)s, to towers of states) and is supported by extensive evidence from string theory.

Distance conjecture. In the moduli space of a theory of quantum gravity, as one moves a geodesic distance \(d \to \infty\), an infinite tower of states becomes exponentially light: \[ m_{\rm tower} \sim m_0 \, e^{-\alpha \, d / M_{\rm Pl}}, \] with \(\alpha\) an order-one constant. This conjecture captures the idea that the EFT description breaks down at infinite distance in moduli space — new light degrees of freedom invalidate the low-energy truncation. In string theory, these towers are typically Kaluza–Klein modes (when an internal cycle decompactifies) or string winding modes (when a cycle shrinks).

De Sitter conjecture. The scalar potential \(V\) in a theory of quantum gravity must satisfy either \[ M_{\rm Pl} \frac{|\nabla V|}{V} \geq c \quad \text{or} \quad M_{\rm Pl}^2 \frac{\min(\nabla_i \nabla_j V)}{V} \leq -c', \] where \(c, c'\) are positive order-one constants. If true, this rules out stable de Sitter vacua in quantum gravity. This is the most controversial swampland conjecture, as it is in tension with the observed dark energy (which behaves like a small positive cosmological constant). The status is actively debated: some constructions (like KKLT) claim to produce de Sitter vacua in string theory, while critics argue these constructions are not fully controlled.

Connections to 2D CFT and modular invariance

The swampland program has deep connections to the worldsheet perspective:

  1. Modular invariance as a swampland constraint. The requirement that the worldsheet CFT be modular invariant is itself a powerful constraint that eliminates many would-be string vacua. The ADE classification of \(SU(2)\) WZW modular invariants is an example of how string consistency drastically limits the allowed theories.

  2. No global symmetries from the worldsheet. On the worldsheet, global symmetries of the spacetime theory arise from worldsheet currents. The requirement that these currents couple to worldsheet gauge fields (from the vertex operators of spacetime gauge bosons) ensures that all spacetime symmetries are gauged.

  3. Orbifolds and discrete gauge symmetries. Orbifold constructions gauge discrete symmetries, producing theories where the discrete symmetry is a gauge symmetry. The completeness hypothesis then predicts that all twisted sectors (carrying discrete gauge charge) must be present — exactly what modular invariance requires.

  4. Distance conjecture and infinite-distance limits in moduli space. In 2D CFT terms, the distance conjecture corresponds to degeneration limits of the worldsheet theory, where the CFT develops a continuum of light states. The distance conjecture has been verified in numerous string compactifications by analyzing the behavior of the worldsheet CFT.
